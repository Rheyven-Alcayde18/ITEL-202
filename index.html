PI 100 · Lesson 7

Noli Me Tangere
Study Reviewer

Touch Me Not  ·  Huwag Mo Akong Salingin

Published Berlin, 1887 Author Dr. José Rizal 63 Chapters + Epilogue

Background & Publication

Origin & Inspiration

  • Rizal was inspired by Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin to write a novel depicting Filipino misery under Spanish tyranny.
  • On January 2, 1884, at a reunion in the Paterno residence in Madrid, Rizal proposed a group novel about the Philippines written by Filipinos.

Writing Timeline

  • Madrid Late 1884 — Began writing; completed roughly the first half.
  • Paris 1885 — After finishing at Central University of Madrid; completed the next quarter.
  • Wilhelmsfield April–June 1886 — Wrote the final chapters.
  • Berlin February 1886 (winter) — Made final revisions on the manuscript.
  • Published February 21, 1887 — Noli finished and ready for printing.

Publication Details

  • Maximo Viola (friend from Bulacan) — loaned Rizal funds to publish the novel; known as the "savior of Noli."
  • Printed at Berliner Buchdruckerei-Actien-Gesselschaft — 300 pesos for 2,000 copies, the cheapest rate.
  • To save expenses, Rizal deleted passages including an entire chapter — "Elias and Salome."
  • Dedicated to the Philippines — "To My Fatherland."
  • Contains 63 chapters and an epilogue. Original copy at the National Library of the Philippines, Ermita, Manila.

About the Title

  • Noli Me Tangere is Latin for "Touch Me Not" — Rizal took it from the Bible.
  • In his March 5, 1887 letter to Felix Hidalgo, Rizal mistakenly attributed it to the Gospel of St. Luke — it actually comes from the Gospel of St. John (Chapter 20, Verses 13–17).
  • English translation: Social Cancer. Filipino: Huwag Mo Akong Salingin.

Cover Symbolism

  • Woman's head (Maria Clara bodice) — the nation and women as victims of social cancer.
  • Friar's outsized feet — the dominant, disproportionate power of the church over society.
  • Guard's helmet & iron chains — oppression and discrimination.
  • Teacher's whip & alférez's scourge — abuses of authority in education and the military.
  • Bamboo cluster — the Filipino people, forever in the background of their own country's history.
  • Cross, maze, flowers, thorns, flame — religious policy, misdirected ardor, and a people strangled by all of these forces.

Characters

JCI
Juan Crisostomo Ibarra
Protagonist; wealthy young Filipino who studied in Europe for 7 years. Fiancé of Maria Clara; dreamt of building a school in San Diego.
MC
Maria Clara
Ibarra's childhood sweetheart. True daughter of Doña Pia Alba and Padre Damaso; raised by Kapitan Tiago.
PD
Padre Damaso
Franciscan friar; parish priest of San Diego for 20 years. Arrogant, domineering, anti-Filipino. Biological father of Maria Clara.
EL
Elias
Ibarra's mysterious friend and fugitive; a boat pilot whom Ibarra saved from a crocodile threat. Voice of the oppressed masses.
KT
Kapitan Tiago
Supposed father of Maria Clara; friend of Don Rafael. Businessman from Binondok; church patron and follower of Padre Damaso.
PS
Padre Salvi
Full name: Bernardo Salví. Secret admirer of Maria Clara. Replaced Padre Damaso as parish priest of San Diego.
PT
Pilosopo Tasio
Don Anastacio — "too advanced for his time." Ibarra consulted him about building a school for San Diego.
SS
Sisa
Narcisa — loving mother of Crispin and Basilio; wife of Pedro. A tragic figure driven to madness by grief and injustice.
CR
Crispin
Sisa's younger son. Falsely accused of stealing from the priest; tortured in the convent and died there. His brother Basilio escaped the belltower.
DV
Doña Victorina
Native woman who scorned her own people, pretending to be a superior Spaniard.
TE
Don Tiburcio de Espadaña
Bogus Spanish physician; lame and henpecked husband of Doña Victorina.
DC
Doña Consolacion
Vulgar mistress of the Alférez; pretended to speak Spanish fluently.
AL
Alférez
Chief of the Guardia Civil; mortal enemy of the priests over control and power in San Diego.
LG
Lt. Guevarra
Elderly, kind lieutenant of the Guardia Civil; revealed to Ibarra the truth of what happened to his father, Don Rafael.
DR
Don Rafael Ibarra
Father of Crisostomo; described as rich and brave. His fate is central to Ibarra's motivations in the novel.
HS
Padre Hernando de la Sibyla
Dominican priest who closely observed the life and actions of Crisostomo Ibarra.

Storm Over the Noli

Government Reaction

  • Gov. Gen. Emilio Terrero summoned Rizal to Malacañang. Rizal said he merely exposed the truth, not advocated subversion.
  • Terrero, curious, asked for a copy. Knowing Rizal's life was in danger from the friars, he assigned Lt. Don Jose Taviel de Andrade as Rizal's bodyguard.
  • Archbishop Msgr. Pedro Payo sent the Noli to UST rector Fr. Gregorio Echavarria for faculty review.
  • UST faculty declared it: "heretical, impious, scandalous, antipatriotic, subversive of public order, and injurious to Spain."
  • Terrero, dissatisfied, sent it to the Permanent Commission of Censorship (headed by Fr. Salvador Font, Augustinian Cura of Tondo). They recommended absolute prohibition of the novel.

Attackers

  • Fr. Font printed and distributed his censorship report widely to discredit the novel.
  • Fr. Jose Rodriguez (Prior of Guadalupe) published 8 pamphlets — Cuestiones de Sumo Interes — attacking the Noli.
  • Attacked in the Spanish Senate (Cortes) by Gen. Jose de Salamanca, Gen. Luis M. Pando, and Fernando Vida.

Defenders

  • Marcelo H. del Pilar, Dr. Antonio Ma. Regidor, Graciano Lopez Jaena, Mariano Ponce — Filipino reformists abroad who defended the novel's truths.
  • Father Sanchez — Rizal's favorite Ateneo teacher; publicly praised and defended it.
  • Rev. Vicente Garcia (pen name: Justo Desiderio Magalang) — wrote a defense published in Singapore on July 18, 1888.
